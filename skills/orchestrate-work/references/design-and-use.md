# Design and use

Use this procedure when the task's uncertainty concerns what to make or how people will experience it. Scale its depth to the request; it is not a mandatory full product process for every change.

## Understand the job

Have research or product analysis distill the brief into the user's situation, important tasks, constraints, and unanswered questions. Preserve evidence and distinguish user statements from inferred preferences. Describe a concrete successful use: what someone should understand, decide, or accomplish with the result.

Let designers challenge product assumptions with reasons. A product brief supplies context and constraints; it should not force every early conjecture into the finished artifact. Return consequential changes to the coordinator rather than silently redefining the product.

## Explore before committing

When direction is still uncertain, develop genuinely different approaches to the important design choice. Different colors on the same generic template are not meaningful alternatives. Show enough of each approach to compare hierarchy, interaction, content, and fit to the user's situation. Stop exploring when there is enough evidence to choose; do not impose a fixed number of proposals.

For visual products, make inspectable designs before committing to broad implementation. A sketch, image, design canvas, or small rendered prototype can serve this purpose. Use available tools such as Paper or Doop when they help; neither is a dependency, and do not install a tool solely because this skill mentions it. An implementation-shaped prototype is appropriate when interaction itself is the uncertainty, provided it remains exploratory rather than an excuse to skip design judgment.

For writing or other artifacts, the equivalent may be competing outlines, sample passages, page compositions, or worked examples. Choose the representation that makes the consequential decisions visible.

## Inspect and select

Inspect the actual proposals at a useful size, with representative content. Judge each against the user's tasks and constraints: what draws attention, what is hard to parse, what action is obvious, and what evidence is missing. A rationale cannot substitute for seeing a visual design. If viewing is unavailable, state that gap and use the best available representation without claiming visual validation.

Have independent reviewers examine distinct useful axes, such as product fit, visual communication, and engineering feasibility. Keep technical feasibility from becoming an automatic veto on a valuable design; identify the concrete constraint and viable alternatives. The assigned outcome owners choose a coherent direction within their mandate, taking shared decisions and scope changes to the coordinator; without delegated ownership, the coordinator makes that choice. Explain important tradeoffs. Do not average incompatible proposals or tally reviewer votes.

Record only the decisions downstream work needs: content priority, intended interactions, visual direction, and accepted compromises. Pass those along with the chosen artifacts to implementation workers so the design is not reduced to a vague adjective.

## Build and experience the result

Implement the selected direction in verifiable slices. Compare the rendered result with the design intent during integration. Test whether representative content, empty states, errors, and constrained viewports change the hierarchy or usefulness when those cases matter. Keep caveats and supporting diagnostics proportionate to their effect on the user's main task; avoid letting them displace the answer or action people came for.

After implementation, give fresh reviewers concrete tasks or persona scenarios and have them actually use the candidate when tools permit. Separate observed behavior from speculation and simulated personas from real user feedback. A dashboard reviewer should attempt to find and interpret the answer, not merely inspect a screenshot and declare it attractive. Engineering checks and visual inspection answer different questions.

Fix the underlying cause of consequential feedback. A confusing primary result may require a new hierarchy or product decision, not another tooltip. Reinspect materially changed designs and rerun affected journeys. End when the result supports the intended use and the remaining concerns are understood, not because every reviewer has found something to praise.
